01

Why memorize Tanya?

In 1796 the Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, published Tanya — the Book of Beinonim — a roadmap for the everyday soul: two souls at war, the beinoni who never surrenders, and a love of G-d that is “very near” to every one of us. Its fifty-three chapters became the foundational text of Chabad Chassidus.

“Tanya is the Written Torah of Chabad Chassidus. Not only is every concept precise and deliberate, but so is every single word, to the last letter.”— Igros Kodesh of the Rebbe Rayatz, Vol. 3, p. 261

Because every word is that precise, chassidim in every generation have treated Tanya with the reverence reserved for a Chumash — and the Rebbe, in letter after letter across four decades, prescribed one practice to Jews of every walk of life: know at least one chapter of Tanya by heart, and review it — aloud or in thought — while walking, traveling, and waiting.

A guarded mind

Words of Torah engraved in memory protect a person even in moments when he is not reviewing them — and a mind filled with holy letters leaves no empty room for unwanted thoughts.

The Rebbe’s letters, e.g. Igros Kodesh vol. 5, 1,457 · vol. 8, 2,525 — citing Likkutei Torah, end of Kedoshim
A weapon at hand

“Learn by heart the beginning of Chapter 41… until those sacred words are engraved upon your mind and memory, so that you will always be able to recall them… whenever the need arises.”

The Rebbe, letter of 13 Teves 5726 — the passage “G-d stands over him…,” his prescription for laziness, anger, sadness, fear, and distracted prayer
Everywhere you go

Know some chapters of Mishnayos and at least one chapter of Tanya by heart, and go over them on the way to work and back — a practice the Rebbe promised brings success in spiritual and material life alike.

The Rebbe’s letters, e.g. Igros Kodesh vol. 21, 8,019 · letter of 18 Sivan 5719

A chapter carried by heart is a chapter that can speak up exactly when the inner battle of Chapter 12 is actually happening. The Rebbe pointed to the known virtue of knowing Tanya itself by heart — recalling the Frierdiker Rebbe’s talk of Simchas Torah 5691 — and encouraged students to master its opening twelve chapters with the introduction.

02

How the book makes it stick

Fifty-three chapters is a forest. The Garden of Tanya clears a path through it with four devices, each doing a different job for your memory:

01

One word per chapter

Every chapter is anchored to a single English keyword — Souls, War, Beinoni, Candle, Joy, Temple — in the spirit of Rabbi Shmuel Yehuda HaLevi Weinfeld’s Simanei Ha’amarim, whose one-word Hebrew headings were designed as symbols and mnemonics.

02

One image per chapter

Each chapter has its own original illustration — a visual scene the mind files far more easily than an abstract idea. Word and picture lock together into one memory.

03

One capsule summary

Each chapter is distilled to a few memorizable lines — small enough to rehearse in a minute, faithful enough to unfold back into the full chapter when you return to the text.

04

One map of the whole

The chapters are grouped into arcs — the two souls, the beinoni, overcoming sadness, love and fear, G-d’s presence — and the entire book is retold in “Tanya in 1000 Words,” so every chapter has an address.

03

The garden, in fifty-three words

Say these in order and you are walking the whole of Likkutei Amarim. This is the deck the game deals from.

The memory game

Now plant them where you’ll keep them.

Flip the cards and match the pairs. Every card is a chapter illustration — and every match reveals its chapter number, keyword, and capsule summary, with a deeper teaching to read before you play on.

  • Three levels — Little Sprouts (ages 4–7), Growing Scholars (8–12), and Teens & Adults, drawing from all 53 chapters.
  • Play in order to learn the sequence, or shuffled to test yourself.
  • Each match is a chapter learned — word, image, and idea together.
